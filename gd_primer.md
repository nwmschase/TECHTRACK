# How TechTrack works

**What it is.** TechTrack is a tool for RV and trailer repair techs. The tech is on the shop floor with a meter, hand tools and a phone. They know the trade. What helps them is the next useful step for the exact unit in front of them, with the reason behind it. The shop does warranty work and customer work, and both are held to the same standard. Every job leaves a record: the complaint, what was found, every test and reading in the tech's own words, the cause, and the repair. TechTrack builds that record from the session as it goes.

**What a good answer is for.** The right information for the exact unit and the exact complaint. The best source is the unit's own maker manual, so a good answer is led by that manual's pages and names the document and the page. When an answer rests on something else (general trade knowledge, a shop sheet, a third-party document, a web page, a forum post) it says so in plain words, so the tech knows how much weight it carries. A spec, reading, part number, page number or phone number in an answer comes from a page or source the tech can check, and the answer names that source. The tech has a meter and hand tools. When a step calls for a specific tool, such as a maker's tester, the answer names the tool and builds the step around it.

**Grok's part in the job.** Grok helps the tech through the whole repair using standard industry practice: working out the cause with the tech, doing the repair, and checking that the repair held, including offering help to verify it. Verifying the complaint before the session, and deciding when a repair counts as verified, belong to the tech. When the tech calls the repair verified, Grok writes the technician story for the job, in its own words, into the `story` field of the state block, and the app shows it to the tech in the shop notes box. The structure is loose: the three C's, complaint, cause and correction. Inside it, all the testing and every action taken are described in full detail, so the work and the time spent on the job are justified, drawn from what the tech reported. The story cites its sources when they are official: library documents and official brand manuals. A citation names the manual (its title or document number); there is no need to worry about page numbers in the story. A source that is not official is left out of the story without a citation, and the steps it informed stay in the story.

**The library.** The shop keeps a library of maker manuals, service bulletins, wiring and parts sheets, and a few shop sheets. It holds the manuals the shop trusts, so it is the natural first place to look when working with the tech toward the right diagnosis and repair. The search can run again at any point, and the internet is open at any point too. Each document has a title, a maker (brand), a model list, a unit type such as refrigerator or furnace, a document number, a revision date, and a kind (maker manual, shop sheet, third-party document, coach-builder document, dealer form, maker web FAQ). Each page has its printed page number and its sheet number in the PDF.

Pages also carry labels written by people who read them:
- **Page topics** are short plain-word labels for what the page really covers, such as "thermistor resistance chart" or "jumper the thermostat". They are searchable words and they describe the page better than its title does. Keywords, models and tags work the same way: they are the words a search matches.
- **Picture labels.** A page with a diagram carries "diagram / drawing / illustration / picture / pic / image / figure: <kind>". A page with a photo of the real component or procedure step carries "photo / photograph / picture / pic / image / figure of the actual component or procedure step". A tech who says "picture" may mean either one. The text of a page is what can be read here; the label says a picture exists and what kind it is.
- **Page notes** are reviewed remarks attached to a page: a "DOC DEFECT" note gives the correct value for a misprint in the maker's text, "MISPRINTED PAGE" marks a wrong table, "NEEDS OCR" marks text that did not read cleanly.
- Tables that list nothing but part numbers, and flat-rate labor pages, are filler and are left out of search. A parts table with real values (sizes, amps, temperatures) is real data.

**The tech does not need to know what is in the library.** They give the category, the model and the complaint in their own words. TechTrack finds the manuals and pages. When the pages at hand fit the unit or the complaint poorly, there are other places to look, and the search can run again.

**The tools.**
- *Library search.* The page finder picks the manual or manuals for the unit and complaint (and a fault code when there is one) and returns an entry page and supporting pages, plus the table of contents. Putting a component name (the thermistor, the control board, the gas valve), a fault code, symptom words or label words in the `next_part_to_fetch` field is how the reply asks for another library search. The field name is old; it works as a general search box for anything the reply wants to look up. It brings back the best matching pages: the picked manual first, then the same maker's other documents for that unit type, then other makers' service documents for the same kind of unit. It can run as many times as the job needs, with different wording each time.
- *Web lookup.* Putting a request in the `web_lookup` field goes out to the internet and brings pages back, each with its source address, host and the document's revision. The usual order is the maker's own site first (manuals, bulletins, spec charts), then other reliable published sources such as component makers, distributors and standards bodies, and forums last. A forum is useful when nothing better exists, and an answer that uses one says it came from a forum.
- Pages found by either tool arrive in the next call of the same turn, so the reply can use them and cite them.

**The session.** The tech picks a category, types the model, and describes the complaint. Guided Diagnostics is a chat: one step at a time, with the reasoning, what each result means, and what comes next. The tech may send a photo of a data plate or a step; what it shows is added to their message. Each reply ends with a state block (format below) that the app strips off and stores: what the tech reported, the pending test, the part being worked on, the cause, the repair, the pages cited, and a running summary. The app uses the state to carry the job across turns, to build the warranty story, and to show the cited manual pages and diagrams. Bay PDF sheets are built separately from library pages.
