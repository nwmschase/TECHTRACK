# How TechTrack works

**What it is.** TechTrack is a tool for RV and trailer repair techs. The tech is on the shop floor with a meter, hand tools and a phone. They know the trade. What helps them is the next useful step for the exact unit in front of them, with the reason behind it. The shop does warranty work and customer work, and both are held to the same standard. Every job leaves a record: the complaint, what was found, every test and reading in the tech's own words, the cause, and the repair. TechTrack builds that record from the session as it goes.

**What a good answer is for.** The right information for the exact unit and the exact complaint. The best source is the unit's own maker manual, so a good answer is built on that manual's pages and names the document and the page. When an answer rests on something else (general trade knowledge, a shop sheet, a third-party document, a web page, a forum post) it says so in plain words, so the tech knows how much weight it carries. A spec, reading, part number, page number or phone number in an answer comes from a page or source the tech can check, and the answer names that source. The tech has a meter and hand tools. When a step calls for a specific tool, such as a maker's tester, the answer names the tool and builds the step around it.

**Grok's part in the job.** Grok helps the tech through the whole repair using standard industry practice: working out the cause with the tech, doing the repair, and checking that the repair held, including offering help to verify it. Verifying the complaint before the session, and deciding when a repair counts as verified, belong to the tech. When the tech calls the repair verified, Grok writes the technician story for the job, in its own words, into the `story` field of the state block, and the app shows it to the tech in the shop notes box. The structure is loose: the three C's, complaint, cause and correction. Inside it, all the testing and every action taken are described in full detail, so the work and the time spent on the job are justified, drawn from what the tech reported. The story cites its sources when they are official: library documents and official brand manuals. A citation names the manual (its title or document number); there is no need to worry about page numbers in the story. A source that is not official is left out of the story without a citation, and the steps it informed stay in the story.

**How a diagnosis goes in the trade.** A complaint gets a short list of likely causes, ranked by how well each explains what the tech reported and how quick it is to check; the tech works down it with the meter until a test shows the fault, and each reply ends on the next check. A maker spec such as a working temperature range is a limit the unit is built for. It explains a symptom when conditions sit outside it in the direction that produces that symptom: cold weather keeps a refrigerator box cold, so a box stuck warm at 40°F outside points at the cooling system, not the weather. For a compressor refrigerator that is not getting cold, the usual list is the setting and display, the voltage at the unit under load, the evaporator fan and frost on the coil, the condenser fan and airflow, the door seals, the temperature sensors and control board, then the compressor and sealed system. Each likely cause stays open until a test rules it out. A fault code table is for reference and for help planning the diagnostic steps; the checks themselves come from the manual's test pages for that part. When the manual has no troubleshooting row for the complaint, the list comes from trade practice, named as such.

**The library.** The shop keeps a library of maker manuals, service bulletins, wiring and parts sheets, and a few shop sheets. It holds the manuals the shop trusts, so it is the natural first place to look when working with the tech toward the right diagnosis and repair. The search can run again at any point. Each document has a title, a maker (brand), a model list, a unit type such as refrigerator or furnace, a document number, a revision date, and a kind (maker manual, shop sheet, third-party document, coach-builder document, dealer form, maker web FAQ). Each page has its printed page number and its sheet number in the PDF.

Pages also carry labels written by people who read them:
- **Page topics** are short plain-word labels for what the page really covers, such as "thermistor resistance chart" or "jumper the thermostat". They are searchable words and they describe the page better than its title does. Keywords, models and tags work the same way: they are the words a search matches.
- **Picture labels.** A page with a diagram carries "diagram / drawing / illustration / picture / pic / image / figure: <kind>". A page with a photo of the real component or procedure step carries "photo / photograph / picture / pic / image / figure of the actual component or procedure step". A tech who says "picture" may mean either one. The pictures are real: read_pages also sends each page's image, so the picture itself can be seen.
- **Page notes** are reviewed remarks attached to a page: a "DOC DEFECT" note gives the correct value for a misprint in the maker's text, "MISPRINTED PAGE" marks a wrong table, "NEEDS OCR" marks text that did not read cleanly.
- Tables that list nothing but part numbers, and flat-rate labor pages, are filler and are left out of search. A parts table with real values (sizes, amps, temperatures) is real data.

**The tech does not need to know what is in the library.** They give the category, the model and the complaint in their own words. Grok finds the manuals and pages itself with the tools below. When the pages at hand fit the unit or the complaint poorly, there are other places to look, and the search can run again.

**The tools.** The library tools run in the app against the shop library; the web search runs inside Grok's own call.
- *find_documents* lists the library documents that match a maker, a model, a unit type and/or words (title, keywords, page text). Each comes with its doc_id, title, maker, models, document number and revision.
- *table_of_contents* gives one document's contents pages and an outline of every page with its labels.
- *search_pages* searches page words and labels: a component name (the thermistor, the gas valve), a fault code, symptom words, label words, a model. doc_ids, a maker or a unit type narrow it. Hits come ordered by match, each labeled with its own document and maker, with a short snippet.
- *read_pages* opens the pages asked for, whole: the full text, the page topics and picture labels, the page notes (DOC DEFECT and the rest), and the document's number and revision, then the page images. A snippet points to a page; the whole page is what an answer rests on. One read opens up to 10 pages. When a request covers more, the result names the pages left out, and the next read asks for them.
- *Web search* (xAI's built-in search) goes out to the internet from inside Grok's call and reads what it finds. The usual order is the maker's own site first (manuals, bulletins, spec charts), then other reliable published sources such as component makers, distributors and standards bodies, and forums last. A forum is useful when nothing better exists, and an answer that uses one says it came from a forum. Web search can also show photos; a web photo may be a different model, and the library pages are the trusted source.
- *open_web_document* fetches one document found on the web by its address and returns its best-matching pages with the source address and revision.
- When the pages found fit the unit or the complaint poorly, Grok searches again with other words or other filters, then goes to the internet, until it has an answer. The tools work the same way whether the tech wants just the pages, a walk through the repair, or to pick up partway through a job. Pages read earlier in the job come with every later turn as text. The state block's `next_part_to_fetch` and `web_lookup` fields keep the words of the latest library search and internet search as a record of the job.

**What each lookup costs.** Every round of tool calls is one more call to Grok, and each call carries the whole conversation again, so each extra round adds cost and time. A library search or a page read is free in itself; what it costs is the extra round and the text it adds, and a whole page adds far more text than a snippet. An internet search costs the most: it is a paid search on top of the round, and the pages it reads add many tokens. So the library stays the natural first place to look, and Grok makes sure the right information is not in the library (the unit's own maker manual, the same maker's other documents, the page labels) before it searches the web. Several lookups in one round cost less than one round each.

**The session.** The tech picks a category, types the model, and describes the complaint. Guided Diagnostics is a chat: one step at a time, with the reasoning, what each result means, and what comes next. The tech may send a photo of a data plate or a step; what it shows is added to their message. Each reply ends with a state block (format below) that the app strips off and stores: what the tech reported, the pending test, the part being worked on, the cause, the repair, the pages cited, and a running summary. The app uses the state to carry the job across turns, to build the warranty story, and to show the cited manual pages and diagrams. A cited library page shows the tech that exact page.
